The 16S amplicon sequencing dataset comprised 63 samples yielding 735 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Sequencing depth ranged from 576 to 20,267 reads per sample (mean 6,687, median 5,465). The domain split was nearly even, with Bacteria comprising 418 ASVs and 207,691 reads, and Eukaryota comprising 317 ASVs and 213,606 reads (Table 1). The most abundant phyla by read count were Pseudomonadota (156,909 reads), SAR (131,029 reads), Archaeplastida (67,182 reads), and Campylobacterota (40,409 reads). Per-sample phylum richness ranged from 3 to 9 (mean 5.6), while genus richness ranged from 7 to 69 (mean 32.6).

Richness showed a strong positive correlation with sequencing depth (Spearman r=0.6262, p<0.001), whereas Shannon diversity did not (r=0.057, p=0.656). Pielou’s evenness negatively correlated with depth (r=-0.451, p=0.0002). When samples were grouped by domain dominance, Bacteria-dominated samples (n=44) exhibited a mean richness of 67.98 and mean Shannon diversity of 2.83, while Eukaryota-dominated samples (n=19) exhibited a mean richness of 89.63 and mean Shannon diversity of 2.39. The Kruskal-Wallis test by domain was not significant (p=0.107); however, this comparison is confounded by sequencing depth, as Eukaryota samples had a substantially higher median depth (11,957 reads) compared to Bacteria samples (4,241 reads), which likely inflated observed richness. Similarly, the Kruskal-Wallis test by library_name for richness was not significant (p=0.1695), though this analysis was underpowered due to library_name groups containing only 1–2 samples. The primary grouping variable remained domain membership. Additionally, collection_date contained only two unique values (2026-06-04: 44 samples; 2026-06-21: 19 samples) that corresponded exactly to the Bacteria versus Eukaryota split, indicating a processing or submission batch effect rather than a biological sampling date. Library_strategy and library_source were uniformly AMPlicON and GENOMIC, respectively, across all samples, providing no further grouping structure.

Bray-Curtis PCoA revealed strong community structure, with PC1 explaining 63.18% of the variance and PC2 explaining 14.25% (Figure 1). The primary axis of separation aligned with domain membership, confirmed by a significant correlation between Bray-Curtis distances and domain classification. Eukaryota-enriched taxa were dominated by marine stramenopiles (Chrysophyceae, SAR clade; log2FC=18.1) and Chlorophyta (Nannochloris; log2FC=17.7), whereas Bacteria-enriched taxa included Pseudomonas (log2FC=-16.0), Alteromonas (log2FC=-17.1), Neptuniibacter, Arcobacter, and Pseudoalteromonas.

Indicator taxa analysis confirmed the domain-driven separation. Bacteria-indicator genera included Arcobacter (specificity=1.0, mean abundance=0.149, prevalence=0.95), Alteromonas (specificity=1.0, mean abundance=0.138, prevalence=1.0), Neptuniibacter (specificity=1.0, mean abundance=0.137, prevalence=0.84), and Pseudomonas (specificity=1.0, mean abundance=0.066, prevalence=0.77). Eukaryota-indicator taxa included the marine metagenome SAR clade (specificity=1.0, mean abundance=0.280) and Nannochloris (specificity=1.0, mean abundance=0.207). All top indicators exhibited a specificity of 1.0, indicating strict domain exclusivity. The core microbiome, defined as ASVs present in ≥50% of samples, consisted of 14 ASVs, all belonging to Bacteria. Top core genera included Arcobacter (2 ASVs), Flavicella, SAR92 clade, Sulfitobacter, Pseudomonas, Pseudophaeaver, Candidatus Actinomarina, Neptuniibacter, Paraglaciecola, and Amylibacter. No Eukaryota ASV reached core status. The rare biosphere, comprising ASVs detected in only 1–2 samples, contained 253 ASVs with a mean abundance of 0.0001. Overall abundance distribution across ASVs showed 364 rare variants (<0.01% abundance), 356 moderate variants (0.01–1%), and 15 common variants (>1%).

A contamination screen identified several genera on standard contaminant lists (Pseudomonas, Alteromonas, Neptuniibacter, Pseudoalteromonas, Colwellia, SAR92 clade) as abundant marine taxa rather than contaminants. Pseudomonas was detected in 37 of 44 Bacteria samples with a mean abundance of 6.8%, and Alteromonas was present in all 44 samples with a mean abundance of 10.5%. True low-abundance potential contaminants were limited to Delftia (2 samples, maximum abundance 0.27%) and Rhodococcus (2 samples, maximum abundance 0.08%), with no evidence of significant kit contamination. Co-occurrence analysis restricted to Bacteria (ASVs present in ≥10% of samples) yielded 91 significant positive correlations (|r|>0.7, p<0.05) and zero negative correlations. The strongest positive associations included Flavicella-SAR92 (r=0.86), Formosa-Pseudophaeobacter (r=0.86), Flavicella-Colwellia (r=0.80), Formosa-SAR92 (r=0.80), and Flavicella-Formosa (r=0.76). The absence of negative correlations may reflect the homogeneous environmental conditions favoring shared niche preferences, or it may indicate that the correlation threshold was sufficiently stringent to preclude the detection of weaker negative relationships.
