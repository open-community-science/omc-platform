## Results

Before addressing community structure, an important data-quality issue emerged from taxonomic classification against the SILVA database. The 63 samples in this dataset separated into two perfectly distinct batches according to SRA accession prefix, and this separation corresponded to domain composition rather than any biological gradient: all 44 samples with the SRR38958xxx prefix were classified as essentially pure Bacteria (>99% of ASVs), while all 19 samples with the SRR38966xxx prefix were classified as essentially pure Eukaryota (fraction of samples showing near-total domain purity = 1.0). Both batches shared the identical SRA description "16S amplicon sequencing," indicating that this label is incorrect for the SRR38966xxx batch, which in fact represents an 18S or other eukaryotic-targeted amplicon run. Consistent with this, pairwise Bray-Curtis dissimilarity between the two batches was at the theoretical maximum (mean = 1.0), reflecting completely disjoint ASV sets by construction, whereas within-batch dissimilarity averaged 0.615 (bacteria) and 0.625 (eukaryote), confirming that the batches represent non-overlapping community tables rather than shared communities differing only in the proportion of eukaryotic reads. Given this, all subsequent analyses were considered separately within each domain batch.

Several additional technical caveats affected interpretation of diversity metrics. ASV richness was strongly correlated with sequencing depth across the full dataset (Spearman rho = 0.626, p < 1e-6), and this relationship was particularly pronounced within the bacterial batch (rho = 0.780, p < 1e-6) though weaker and non-significant within the eukaryote batch (rho = 0.382, p = 0.107), indicating that raw richness comparisons across samples would largely track depth rather than ecological differences. Pielou evenness showed the converse pattern, declining significantly with increasing depth both overall (rho = -0.451, p = 0.0002) and within the bacterial batch (rho = -0.455, p = 0.0019), an artifact consistent with deeper samples revealing more low-abundance ASVs that mathematically depress evenness. Ordination axes were also affected: within the eukaryote batch, the primary ordination coordinate was very strongly correlated with richness (rho = -0.879, p < 1e-6), and a weaker but still significant depth/richness association was present on the corresponding bacterial-batch axis (rho = 0.487, p = 0.0008, versus n_asvs; rho = 0.404, p = 0.0065, versus depth), suggesting that at least part of the separation visualized in ordination space reflects sequencing depth rather than pure compositional variation. The ASV table had already been filtered to remove singletons and very rare ASVs (minimum 3 total reads and prevalence in ≥2 samples; 0 singleton ASVs remained), so classical rare-biosphere analyses based on singleton counts were not applicable to this table.

Despite these caveats, clear and statistically robust ecological structure was evident within each domain batch. Habitat type (frost flower, brine, drained ice, whole core, air, sea water) explained a large proportion of community variance in both the bacterial batch (PERMANOVA F = 14.61, p = 0.001, n = 44, 6 groups) and the eukaryote batch (F = 10.52, p = 0.001, n = 18, 4 groups), indicating that habitat-driven compositional differences were not simply an artifact of the domain/batch split. In the bacterial batch, community composition was moderately dominated by a small number of taxa overall: the single most abundant ASV (out of 735 total ASVs) accounted for 15.4% of all reads, and the top 10 ASVs together accounted for 59.5% of reads, with the leading ASVs assigned to Chrysophyceae, Nannochloris-like green algae, Arcobacter, Neptuniibacter, and Alteromonas. At the per-sample level, the dominant ASV averaged 35.0% of reads (up to 83.2% in the most dominated sample), a pattern consistent with the bloom-like dominance typical of sea-ice microbial communities.

This dominance was structured by habitat (Table/Figure showing per-habitat genus composition). Frost flower samples were dominated by Neptuniibacter (mean relative abundance 41.0%) alongside Alteromonas; drained ice samples were dominated by Arcobacter (52.3%); whole core samples were dominated by Pseudomonas (53.4%); and brine samples showed a more even distribution among Alteromonas (27.3%), Arcobacter (18.7%), Pseudomonas (15.4%), and Pseudoalteromonas (14.8%). These same five genera — Arcobacter, Neptuniibacter, Alteromonas, Pseudomonas, and Pseudoalteromonas — also emerged as the core resident taxa of the bacterial community, ranking highest by total read count (33,682; 29,195; 28,717; 12,316; and 6,643 reads, respectively) and present across the majority of samples, indicating that habitat-dominant taxa were the same organisms comprising the persistent core community rather than habitat-exclusive specialists. Differential abundance testing (Kruskal-Wallis) across the four well-replicated habitat types confirmed that habitat structured relative abundance for over half of tested genera (47 of 92 genera significant at uncorrected p < 0.05), with Colwellia showing the strongest habitat association (H = 33.67, p ≈ 0), followed by Neptuniibacter, Marinomonas, Pseudoalteromonas, and the SAR92 clade.

Despite this dominance by a few taxa, the overall occupancy structure of the bacterial ASV table was strongly right-skewed. Only 14 of 735 ASVs (1.9%) met a core-taxon threshold of ≥50% sample prevalence, while 511 ASVs (69.5%) were transient, occurring in ≤10% of samples — indicating a long-tailed rare biosphere that persisted even after pre-filtering had already removed all singleton ASVs.

Finally, screening for classic kit/reagent contaminant genera (Ralstonia, Bradyrhizobium, Cutibacterium, Pelomonas, Delftia, Sphingomonas) found minimal evidence of contamination: five of the six genera were entirely absent, and Delftia was represented by only a single ASV with 16 total reads, detected in just 2 of 63 samples. The relative abundance of this putative contaminant did not correlate with sequencing depth (rho = -0.015, p = 0.906), which would be expected if it were associated with low-biomass samples, further supporting the interpretation that reagent contamination was minimal in this dataset.
